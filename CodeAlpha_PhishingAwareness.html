What is phishing?
A social engineering attack that tricks people into revealing sensitive information or downloading malware.
How a phishing attack unfolds
1
Crafting the bait — The attacker creates a convincing fake email, SMS, or website that impersonates a trusted source (bank, employer, government).
2
Delivering the hook — The victim receives the message with urgent language: "Your account will be suspended," "Verify immediately," "You have a package."
3
The click — Panicked by urgency, the victim clicks a malicious link or opens a weaponized attachment without verifying its authenticity.
4
Data theft / compromise — Credentials, financial data, or access tokens are stolen. Malware may be installed silently for future exploitation.
Why it works

Phishing exploits fundamental human psychology — fear, urgency, authority, and curiosity. Even security-aware individuals can be fooled when attackers have researched their target and crafted a highly personalized message. Awareness is the most effective defense.

Types of phishing attacks
Attackers use many vectors beyond email. Know them all.

Email phishing

Mass fake emails impersonating banks, services, or employers sent to millions of addresses.

Most common

Spear phishing

Targeted attack using personal details — name, job title, colleagues — to appear highly legitimate.

High success rate

Whaling

Targets C-suite executives (CEOs, CFOs) for large-scale financial fraud or data breaches.

High-value target

Smishing

Phishing via SMS messages — fake delivery alerts, bank warnings, or OTP requests with malicious links.

SMS-based

Vishing

Voice call scams impersonating IT support, banks, or government agencies to extract credentials.

Voice-based

Pharming

Redirects users to fake websites through DNS poisoning — even typing the correct URL can be compromised.

Hard to detect
Spot the red flags
Analyze this real-world phishing email — can you find all 5 red flags?
Inbox — Mail Client ⚠ Suspicious
From
security-alert@paypa1.support-center.com
Subject
⚠️ URGENT: Your PayPal account has been compromised — verify NOW

Dear Valued Customer,


We have detected unusual login activity from an unrecognized device on your PayPal account. Your account will be permanently suspended within 24 hours unless you verify your identity immediately.


Click the secure link below to restore full access to your account:


http://paypal-secure-verify.xyz/login?token=829xAB&user=validate


Failure to verify will result in permanent account termination and loss of all funds.


Regards,
PayPal Security Team

🚩 5 red flags identified
⚠
Fake sender domain — "paypa1.support-center.com" uses the number "1" instead of the letter "l". This typosquatting technique is designed to fool a quick glance. PayPal's real domain is paypal.com — nothing else.
⏰
Artificial urgency and fear — "24 hours", "permanently suspended", "loss of all funds" are classic psychological pressure tactics. Attackers create panic so you act before you think. Legitimate companies do not threaten customers this way.
🔗
Malicious URL — "paypal-secure-verify.xyz" is absolutely not PayPal's website. Attackers register domains that sound official. Always check the actual domain — in this case .xyz, not paypal.com.
👤
Generic greeting — "Dear Valued Customer" instead of your actual name signals a mass phishing campaign. Legitimate businesses that have your account always address you by name.
💬
Threatening, coercive language — Threatening "permanent termination" and "loss of funds" is a hallmark of phishing. Legitimate institutions have dispute and recovery processes — they don't issue ultimatums via email.
Best practices to stay safe
Seven habits that dramatically reduce your risk of being phished.
Hover before you click
Hover over any link to preview the real destination URL in your browser's status bar. If it doesn't exactly match the sender's official domain, do not click. When in doubt, go directly to the website by typing the URL yourself.
Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA)
MFA adds a critical second layer of defense. Even if attackers steal your password, they cannot log in without the second factor (authenticator app, SMS code, hardware key). Enable it on every account that supports it.
Verify urgent requests through official channels
If you receive an urgent request — from your bank, IT team, or a colleague — call them back directly using an official phone number you already know. Never use the contact details provided in a suspicious email.
Never open unexpected attachments
Malware is frequently delivered as invoices, delivery notices, or Word/Excel files. Never open attachments from unknown senders. Even from known contacts, be cautious if the message is unexpected — their account may be compromised.
Check the full domain — not just HTTPS
HTTPS and the padlock only mean the connection is encrypted — they do not confirm the site is legitimate. Phishing sites routinely use HTTPS. Always verify the complete domain name carefully, character by character.
Report phishing immediately
Use your email client's "Report Phishing" feature. In a workplace, alert your IT/security team immediately — a phishing attempt targeting you may also target colleagues. Reporting helps protect everyone.
Keep all software updated
Security patches fix the vulnerabilities that phishing attacks often exploit. Enable automatic updates for your OS, browser, and email client. Unpatched software is an open door for attackers.
