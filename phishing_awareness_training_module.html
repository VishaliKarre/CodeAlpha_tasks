What is phishing?

A social engineering attack that tricks people into revealing sensitive information.

How it works
1 Attacker crafts a convincing fake email, message, or website
2 Victim is lured with urgency, fear, or tempting offers
3 Victim clicks a malicious link or downloads malware
4 Attacker steals credentials, money, or data

Spot the red flags

Here is a real-world phishing email example. Can you spot what's wrong?

From
security-alert@paypa1.support-center.com
Suspicious
Subject
⚠️ URGENT: Your account has been compromised — verify NOW

Dear Valued Customer,

We have detected unusual login activity on your PayPal account. Your account will be permanently suspended within 24 hours unless you verify your identity immediately.

Click the link below to restore access:

http://paypal-secure-verify.xyz/login?token=829xA

Failure to verify will result in account termination and loss of funds.

PayPal Security Team

Red flags in this email
Fake sender domain — "paypa1.support-center.com" uses a "1" instead of "l" and is not PayPal's real domain (paypal.com).
Urgency and fear tactics — "24 hours", "permanently suspended", "loss of funds" — designed to panic you into acting fast without thinking.
Suspicious URL — "paypal-secure-verify.xyz" is not PayPal's website. Attackers use similar-looking domains to fool victims.
Generic greeting — "Dear Valued Customer" instead of your real name. Legitimate companies personalize emails.
Threatening language — Threats of account termination or fund loss are classic scare tactics. Legit companies don't threaten customers this way.

Best practices to stay safe

Follow these to protect yourself and your organization.

Hover before you click
Always hover over links to preview the real URL. If it doesn't match the sender's domain, do not click.
Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Even if credentials are stolen, MFA prevents attackers from logging in without the second factor.
Verify requests through official channels
If your bank or colleague requests something urgent, call them directly using a known number — not the one in the email.
Never download unexpected attachments
Malware is often disguised as invoices, delivery notices, or office documents. Scan all attachments before opening.
Check for HTTPS — but don't stop there
HTTPS shows encryption, but phishing sites use HTTPS too. Always verify the full domain name carefully.
Report suspicious emails
Use your email client's "Report Phishing" button. In organizations, report to your IT/security team immediately.
Keep software updated
Security patches fix vulnerabilities that phishing attacks exploit. Enable automatic updates on all devices.
